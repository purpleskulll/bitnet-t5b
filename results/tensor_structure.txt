TENSOR STRUCTURE -- how many bits a ternary weight really needs,
measured on the shipped weights
===========================================================================
  Reproduce: python3 tools/tensor_structure.py

  Model  : ggml-model-i2_s.gguf, microsoft/bitnet-b1.58-2B-4T-gguf
           bitnet-b1.58, GGUF v3, 332 tensors, data at 8351360
  Method : GGUF tensor table parsed with tools/gguf_inspect.py; codes read from
           the file. No inference run, nothing sampled.
  Layout : an i2_s tensor is exactly nelem/4 code bytes; the gap to the next
           tensor is [32] bytes for every i2_s tensor (one f32 scale +
           GGUF alignment), so no scale byte is ever counted as a weight.

  Question. i2_s spends 2.00 bits/weight. log2(3) = 1.585 is the order-0 floor
  for three symbols and a dense base-3 pack reaches 1.600. Is there structure
  -- neighbour correlation, zero clustering, per-block skew -- worth a more
  complicated decoder? Every number below is counted over bytes read from the
  GGUF in full. Nothing is sampled or extrapolated.

  Codes: 0 -> w=-1, 1 -> w=0, 2 -> w=+1, 3 -> unused. "Nonzero" = code != 1.

  Two orders are reported because i2_s is interleaved: a 32-byte block holds
  128 weights, byte j carrying weights j, 32+j, 64+j, 96+j (bits 7-6, 5-4,
  3-2, 1-0). Confirmed against the verbatim upstream AVX2 kernel in
  src_modifications/ggml_i2s_ternary.c, where the >>6/>>4/>>2/>>0 lanes are
  paired with activations py+0/+32/+64/+96 while px advances 32 bytes per 128
  activations. "Storage order" is the byte stream; "row-major" is logical
  weight order with row-straddling pairs excluded.

  The estimator was made to fail on purpose before any null was believed:
  on synthetic i.i.d. symbols H0-H1 = 0.00000 (must be 0), and with half
  the symbols copying their predecessor H0-H1 = 0.08465 (must be large).
  Run as --self-test; both are asserted on every run.

7. VERDICT  (question 6)
---------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Reference point for 'is it worth a decoder': the shipped i2_s kernel
  sustains 20.19 GB/s of weight bytes at a DRAM-resident working set --
  33,554,432 weights in 0.415 ms, i.e. 262,144 blocks of 128 in 0.415 ms
  = 632 million blocks/s (results/bench_code_kernel.txt, rows=8192).
  A replacement decoder must hold that while doing strictly more per
  block than a shift and a mask.

  5 of the 5 schemes DO beat 1.600 bits/weight model-wide, the
  best by 5.11%. None beats it by enough to be worth building.

  Best measured: (d) enumerative, B=128 at 1.5182 bits/weight,
  5.11% below dense base-3 and 24.09% below the shipped 2.000.
  In bytes on this 2B model that is 20.3 MiB saved against base-3,
  on top of the 99.4 MiB that base-3 already saves against i2_s.

  The numbers behind that:

  - Order-1 context buys 0.00694 bits/weight (0.434%), and over live rows
    only 0.00076 (0.047%). The weights are serially independent in
    storage order and in row-major order alike; what the order-1 number
    does show is the dead rows being trivially predictable, not a
    weight-to-weight relationship. A context model is ruled out outright.
  - Zero-skip flags: 1.5672 at their best (B=160), 2.05% below 1.600,
    but they LOSE at small blocks (1.7571 at B=4) because break-even
    needs an all-zero fraction above 1/(1.6*B) = 15.6% at B=4, 0.49% at
    B=128. Every bit of their win comes from the dead rows, not from
    local sparsity -- which is why bigger blocks are strictly better and
    why (f), one flag per ROW, gets nearly the same for far less work.
  - Order-0 arithmetic: 1.5553 per tensor (2.80%), 1.5603 with one
    global table (2.48%). Both need a serial range decoder, whose
    dependency chain cannot be made to run at 632M blocks/s. Paying that
    to save 2.5% of bytes on a bandwidth-bound kernel is a bad trade.
  - The one real structure is per-row sparsity: 19,847 of 683,520
    rows (2.90%) are entirely zero, up to 43.59% of a single
    tensor, and the surviving rows differ widely in density. It is worth
    2.42% as a row skip bit (f) and 3.28% as a per-row table (e).
  - The enumerative scheme (d) is the only one past 3.3%, at 5.11%, and it is
    the most expensive to decode of all of them: a binomial rank/unrank
    per block. It is the clearest case of the pattern -- the more of the
    structure a scheme captures, the less it can keep up with the kernel.

  So the practical ceiling on a repacking effort is the 20.00% between
  the shipped 2.000 and dense base-3 at 1.600. base-3 decodes with a
  multiply and a shift and stays vectorisable; everything below it here
  is worth at most a further 5.11% and costs a decoder that is no
  longer memory-bound, which defeats the purpose on a kernel whose whole
  problem is bytes. If the dead rows are worth having, the way to take
  them is to drop those rows from the model, not to entropy-code them.
